# How I got here 🧭

My career path looks a little nonlinear, but there's a pretty consistent thread running through it: I've always been interested in understanding how things work and then figuring out how to make them better.

It started in my parents' basement.

## I learned to code to solve a problem

When I was 12, I was playing [Habbo Hotel](https://www.habbo.com/), which had a huge fan-site community. I started learning HTML and CSS because I wanted to participate in that community in more interesting ways.

Before long, I noticed that a lot of people were running online radio stations, and there was a need for software to manage their DJs.

So I taught myself PHP and MySQL and [built it](https://github.com/quickscriptz/radio-dj-panel).

That was my first real experience of programming as a way to solve a problem. The technology was interesting, but what really hooked me was that I could see something that wasn't working well, learn whatever I needed to learn, and build something that made it better.

I've been doing some variation of that ever since.

## Then I studied psychology

At the end of high school, I chose to study psychology.

I'd always been fascinated by why people think and behave differently — how two people can encounter the same situation and somehow construct completely different experiences of it.

At the time, I also thought computer science was basically math, and I didn't want to turn something I loved doing into homework.

So I kept programming on the side while I studied something that had always fascinated me: people.

In retrospect, that was a pretty useful detour.

## Eventually, the two came back together

I eventually moved into professional software engineering, building on years of self-taught and freelance development.

Along the way, I got into UX, taught coding bootcamps, and helped create and teach a full-stack web development curriculum.

Teaching was particularly formative. It made me think less about knowledge as a collection of facts and more about how concepts connect to one another.

That turns out to be surprisingly similar to how I think about software architecture: you need to understand the bigger picture before you can figure out where the individual pieces belong.

It also gave me another way to combine the things I'd become interested in — technology, people, communication, and learning.

## The connective bit

As my career progressed, I found myself increasingly drawn to the spaces between disciplines.

I like understanding how a technical decision affects the product, how a product decision affects the engineers building it, and how all of those decisions ultimately affect the people using and operating the system.

I'm often the person who ends up connecting those dots: noticing that a technical change has implications for another team, that a seemingly simple requirement hides an important assumption, or that everyone in a room is using the same words to mean slightly different things.

It's why I've ended up enjoying roles that let me move between writing code, helping teams make technical decisions, teaching other engineers, and thinking about the bigger organizational picture.

The problems are considerably bigger than the ones I was solving at 12. The basic instinct is pretty much the same, though: **get curious about something, figure out how it works, try to make it better, and then help other people understand what you learned.**
